The ensemble you didn't have to train

#ensemble

You have trained one network. You would like to know how the other networks trained on the same data would behave: not an independent ensemble, but the runs that differ from yours only by a relabelling of their units. Their mean weights, their spread, the mean and spread of their gradients. The obvious answer — train a thousand of them — costs a thousand times as much to learn something you could have derived.

Make it precise. Suppose the optimizer's update commutes with the group, in the sense that the run started at is the run started at with applied to every iterate. Then, on the same data in the same order, the whole trajectory from is the image under of the trajectory from . The ensemble of models obtained by relabelling is therefore exactly the orbit of the single model you have.

For any vector that transforms with the group — the weights and the gradient in particular — the ensemble mean and covariance are the orbit averages

with . These are exact group averages, not Monte Carlo estimates (orbit averages are invariant), and they are projections (R1, R2).

The construction is Bernacchia's (2025), who built it to obtain ensemble statistics from a single model and named the ensemble covariance of the gradient the global curvature of the loss. The 2026 paper takes that object seriously as a Hessian estimate; that is the next trail.

The one dynamical assumption is the commuting optimizer. The one interpretive caveat is that the orbit is narrower than a real ensemble. The trailhead for this material is the ensemble trail.
