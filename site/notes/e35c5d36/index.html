The δ/1 basis: two patterns, two terms

#algebra

The invariant operator on the example has the form

with and two matrices on the free axis. Each term is a sparse binary tensor: a Kronecker product of an identity (or an all-ones matrix) on the permuted axis with a small free matrix. That is the form a compiler would generate, because each basis element is easy to apply with an einsum.

The two basis elements answer the two possible questions about the pair of unit indices: "are they the same?" () and "what is the aggregate over all pairs?" (). In the commutant language, the algebra of the permuted axis is spanned by and — dimension two (the commutant algebra). That is why there are exactly two free matrices, not more.

Two group choices make the basis cheaper. Adding sign flips kills the -term and leaves one block (signed permutations). Permuting both axes replaces the pair of matrices by four scalars (both axes).

For linear algebra, the δ/1 basis is not the most convenient one: the two elements are not orthogonal, because a delta overlaps a constant. The normal equations therefore involve a Gram matrix (the Gram matrix). The change to the orthogonal-ish Π/Π⊥ basis is elementary and is the subject of the Π/Π⊥ basis.
