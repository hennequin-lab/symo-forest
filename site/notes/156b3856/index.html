The group is a hyperparameter of the approximation

#optimization#symmetry

The framework's dial is "which group do you declare". At one end is the trivial group, where

a rank-one object that is useless as a preconditioner. At the other end is the full symmetry of the architecture, where the algebra is tiny. In between sits a specific and empirically good choice, and it has a name: Muon (Muon).

The trade-off is two-sided. Larger groups mean a smaller algebra — fewer numbers to store and invert — and a longer orbit — a blunter, less local estimate. Smaller groups mean a shorter orbit and a more local estimate, but more numbers and a worse-conditioned problem. The cost-benefit curve happens to be good near the signed-permutation setting, which is also where a single matrix survives.

Two consequences follow, and the author finds them more interesting than the equivalence itself:

  • Muon's restriction is a choice, and a conservative one. It declares the identity group on some layers, which shortens the orbit, and signed permutations on others. In the framework's language it gives up information in exchange for locality.
  • Every intermediate setting is available. You can keep two blocks separate instead of forcing them equal, or tie a second axis, and pay in proportion. The paper's Fig. 6 explores exactly this, and it is the part the author would most like to see pushed further: the choice of group is a hyperparameter of the approximation, and at the moment it is chosen by taste.

A principle for choosing the group would turn a hyperparameter into a design decision; that is an open question.
