Two covariances and one curvature

#curvature

We have the mean and covariance of the parameters over the ensemble, and of the gradient. What are they good for?

The weight covariance describes the ensemble. The gradient covariance describes how much the gradient moves as you move around it, and the name global curvature is apt once you see it as a finite difference: measures how far apart the members of the ensemble are in parameter space, measures how far apart their gradients are, and the ratio of the two is a curvature. In one dimension that ratio is all there is to it; in dimensions the same statement becomes a matrix equation.

The easy derivation is the delta method. If the gradient varies approximately linearly with the parameters around the centroid, with the Hessian there, then passing a covariance through a linear map gives

That is the content. The two covariances are exact, being group averages computed in closed form, and the approximation is entirely in the linearisation. It is a wide one: the displacement whose covariance is being propagated is the radius of the orbit, not an infinitesimal. Hence the word "global" (the wide-baseline secant).

The unknown Hessian appears twice, between two quantities computable from a single gradient. That is the whole trick. The derivation without covariances is the paper's version; the linear-map version is the delta method; the scalar check is one dimension.

There is a structural catch: is the outer product of a single displacement, so it is severely rank-deficient. That is the subject of why the paper drops it.
