In one dimension the curvature is sqrt(s_g/s_w)

#curvature

In one dimension the equation has the obvious solution

the curvature is the ratio of how much the gradient varies across the orbit to how much the parameters vary across the orbit. That is a completely reasonable thing to call a curvature estimate, and it is what a finite-difference secant would give you if you could afford a step the size of the orbit (the wide-baseline secant).

The check is worth doing because it separates two questions that the matrix formula can obscure. The first is whether the ratio is the right notion of curvature at all; the scalar case says yes, it is the secant slope. The second is whether the group-average machinery preserves that interpretation in higher dimensions; the matrix case says it does, up to the choice of matrix square root (the PSD solution).

It also shows why the sign of the curvature is not identified. A covariance ratio is non-negative, so the estimate gives in one dimension, not . In the matrix case the PSD solution is positive semi-definite by construction. The estimate is a magnitude of curvature, not a signed Hessian; this matters for optimization (preconditioning) and for the fidelity comparison (how good is the estimate).
