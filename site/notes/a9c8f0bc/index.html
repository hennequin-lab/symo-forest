One backward pass gives the whole orbit

#symmetry

At a point you compute once. At every other point of the orbit, equivariance says the gradient is . No extra backward pass, no perturbed parameters, no finite differences.

The cost of "sampling" the orbit is therefore one application of a group element per sample — and for the structured groups in this forest, even that is avoidable, because the group average of those rotated gradients has a closed form. You never enumerate elements. The enumeration is a proof device; the computation is an einsum.

This is the first place where the framework differs from ordinary ensemble methods. Training models costs times as much and gives you samples. Here the "ensemble" is the orbit of one model, and its statistics are exact functions of one gradient.

What the single gradient does not give you is the loss at other orbit points (it is the same, by definition) or the Hessian (that is the approximation in two covariances). The free object is the first derivative around the orbit, not the second.

The next step is to average these rotated gradients. That average is a projection (orbit averages are invariant), and its closed form starts with the average of a permutation.
